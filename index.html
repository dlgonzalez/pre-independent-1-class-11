Reading: Confirmation Bias

We receive 34 gigabytes of information every single day. How does our brain decide what is true and what is fake? It uses shortcuts, and sometimes, those shortcuts are dangerous.

Confirmation Bias

Scientists believe that the average person receives about 34 gigabytes of information and sees or hears 100,000 words every day. That's a lot of information, and no one can possibly concentrate on all of it. We choose what we pay attention to and what we think is important. That creates a problem: confirmation bias. We like to be right, and so we listen to evidence that supports what we already believe and we ignore evidence that goes against what we believe.

For example, if you believe that the world is generally getting worse, you will see only evidence to support your opinion when you hear the news. You will pay attention to stories about rising crime and environmental problems. And you will ignore evidence that suggests people around the world are getting richer, becoming healthier, and living longer.

Confirmation bias means we see what we want to see. It can create problems. For instance, businesses may try a new strategy. The managers will notice positive results, such as increased sales, before they notice negative ones, such as increased costs. That could be expensive for the business.

Social media and other websites can make confirmation bias worse. Groups of online friends agree with each other. They easily share information and news stories that support their opinions. They rarely hear the other side of the argument and miss relevant information. Some people are worried that this kind of "social media bubble" is bad for society.

Is there anything you can do to question your own confirmation bias? You can make an effort to listen to people with different views. You can visit websites that present a different opinion from yours. As soon as you do that, you will learn something about how other people think.

Real World Bias

Discuss these two real-world examples with your group. How does confirmation bias play a role in each situation?

Case 1: The Rival Team Foul

During a football match, a player from Team A tackles a player from Team B. The referee gives a red card to Team A.

Fans of Team A scream that the referee is blind and the tackle was clean. Fans of Team B cheer and say it was the most violent foul they have ever seen.

"Did they see two different tackles? How is confirmation bias changing what their eyes actually see?"
Case 2: The Social Media Algorithm

Maria clicks 'Like' on a video that says eating only meat is the healthiest diet. The algorithm immediately shows her 50 more videos of doctors saying vegetables are toxic.

She reads the comments, and everyone agrees with the doctors. She decides to stop eating vegetables completely.

"What is the 'Social Media Bubble' here? What is the danger of an algorithm that only shows you what you already like?"

Grammar Core: Future Time Clauses

Look at this sentence from the text: "You will learn something about how other people think as soon as you do that." Why didn't they say "as soon as you will do that"? Let's learn the golden rule of future clauses.

The Golden Rule of Time Clauses 🥇

When we talk about two events in the future, the clause with the "time word" does NOT use will. It uses the Simple Present.

Main Clause

WILL + Verb

"I will call you..."

+

Time Word

WHEN
AS SOON AS
BEFORE
AFTER
UNTIL

+
NO
WILL!

Time Clause

Simple Present

"...when I get home."

*Note: You can flip the sentence! "When I get home, I will call you." The rule stays the same: the "when" part stays present tense!

Speaking: The Bias Detective Agency

It's time to put your grammar and analytical skills to the test. You work for an agency that helps people escape their "Social Media Bubbles." Analyze your clients, predict their future behavior, and create a rescue plan!

Step-by-Step

  1. Analyze: Read the Client Profile with your team. What is their specific confirmation bias?
  2. Predict: Use Future Time Clauses to predict what they will do when they see new information. (e.g., "When she reads the news, she will...")
  3. Intervene: Create a 3-step plan to break their bubble using Time Clauses. (e.g., "As soon as he opens his phone, we will...")
Client File 01

"The Doomsday Scroller"

Subject: Mark (Age 34)

Mark believes the world economy is collapsing and society is getting worse every day. He follows 50 "doom-news" accounts. If a friend sends him an article showing that global poverty is actually decreasing, Mark says, "That's fake news created by the government," and deletes it. He spends 6 hours a day reading about disasters.
